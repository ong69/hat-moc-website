How to choose Vietnamese coffee beans for home

A practical guide to roast, grind and flavour before buying your next bag.

If you have ever bought coffee that smelled wonderful but tasted flat at home, the beans might not have been the problem. The roast, grind and brewing method need to work together. A coffee described as ‘strong’ may refer to flavour, bitterness, body or caffeine. Those are not the same thing. Before buying, decide whether you want a bold black coffee, a mellow morning cup or a concentrated brew that still tastes like coffee after you add condensed milk.

Robusta versus Arabica: what will you notice?

Vietnam is especially well known for Robusta. Compared with many Arabica coffees, Robusta often tastes fuller, earthier and more bitter, with less bright acidity. It also generally contains more caffeine. That does not automatically make one bean better. If you like the powerful flavour of traditional cà phê sữa đá, Robusta may be exactly what you want. If you prefer floral or fruit-forward notes, look for a blend or a different origin. Roast level can amplify or soften these differences, so avoid judging by bean type alone.

Whole beans or ground coffee?

Whole beans usually give you more control because you can grind just before brewing. Pre-ground coffee is convenient, but only if the grind suits your equipment. A phin generally needs a grind that allows water to pass slowly without clogging. Espresso-fine coffee can stall a phin, while very coarse grounds may produce a weak, fast drip. If you do not own a grinder, ask what brewing method the ground coffee is intended for rather than choosing a bag solely by its flavour description.

What to check before you pay

Look for the coffee's origin, bean type, roast or packing information, weight and storage advice. Check the price per 100 g when comparing different bag sizes. Beware of claims such as ‘100% authentic’ if they do not explain what is actually in the bag. If you want a particular flavour, ask whether the coffee contains added flavouring or is simply roasted coffee beans. A clear, specific product description is more useful than a dramatic slogan.

A simple first-brew test

Start with a consistent dose and water amount, then change one variable at a time. For a phin, try roughly 20 g of coffee and 80–100 mL of hot water as a starting point, adjusting for your filter. Taste the coffee black first, even if you normally add milk. If it tastes thin, try a finer grind or a slightly higher dose. If it is harsh and slow to drip, try a coarser grind. Keep a note of what worked; the second cup is usually better than the first.

The bottom line

Buy for the drink you actually enjoy, not the fanciest label. A suitable grind, a sensible roast and a little experimentation will do more for your daily cup than a long list of marketing promises.
